suavely alienating.
i don't really like talking. i prefer writing. or just staring awkwardly into the abyss. i don't have coherent thoughts. i can't bring sense into anything. i have to write. then delete. then rewrite. take a break. smoke a cigarette. and then write again. but you can't do that while talking.
it's been hard for me to warm up to strangers. talking with them makes me nervous. i don't know how to make an idea sound good. it's just a train of thoughts. i stop. give too many details. forget parts that my brain deemed important. everyone loses interest. can't blame them.
so i'm just there. it doesn't matter where. on a lunch break at work. in between classes at college. i'm present with my body. fixating on a building. or a car. but mentally, i'm talking. stating opinions. joking. it's a limbo.
kid a feels like this experience to me. being trapped in your own mind. unable to speak. but still watching. even though you're very close, it feels so far away. and it only gets further.
it's not amongst my favourite records. i think i can blame thom yorke. i don't really like him. or his vocals. too whiny. they're supposed to be colder. but the instrumentals bring a false sense of calm. while drifting farther.

Please do not eat me alive, but honestly, this did not really live up to the hype I expected it to. I have been amazed by Radiohead time and time again, and I saw this album at a flea market on vinyl, so naturally I grabbed it. After some googling, I was aware that this is their attempt at ambient music, and a great one at that. I have rarely listened to ambient music, but the little I did enjoy, so I thought this would be just like them. I am not saying I did not enjoy it, I thoroughly did, but out of all their records I have heard or owned, this one is the weakest, with pretty much no competition so far, as I have not heard their entire discography yet. Production and mixing-wise, it’s incredible. The sounds are crisp, clean in skill, everything feeling precise, like it was surgicaly comprised. There is rarely any truly flashy or impressive performance, but that would ruin the point. This music is made to make you feel enveloped, not to blow you away with some loud solos or attempts at attention-stealing. Thom's vocals are, as always, one of a kind, even though he also does not allow himself to truly belt out his skills, mostly relying on his grieving falsetto and some vocal effects. It all just sounds incredible. What also fascinates me, and proves just how effective this album is in ambience, are the feelings it brings out in me. To be truly moved by music is a weirdly hard feat for me, but this album does something not many, pretty much none, have done before. I do not really feel sadness, but fear. The music makes me uncomfortable and anxious in a way horror movies do, and I love horror, so I love whatever this album does with me. But even after all this praise, all the masterful, simple arrangements, after emotions being expressed through sound rather than words, the pure enjoyability of this album is quite low. It’s just not a record I could listen to in my day-to-day life, because it’s best enjoyed when you are focused, and that just feels like too much sometimes. The sound is so sophisticated that I cannot truly appreciate it while driving in a car, showering or any other activity accompanied by music, and that is a criterion truly important for me.

I should also state that the album does feel slightly top-heavy, as maybe the content gets diluted towards the end, as it never really changes. With that, Everything in Its Right Place is a really solid opener. The electronic, glitchy vocals are really cool, the lyrics are very bare in vocabulary count, but the few lines that get repeated are quite interesting, especially the lemon thing, and the entire sound is just so fascinating. Kid A continues such a vibe with even more electronic, vocoded vocals and, once again, very few sentences being repeated. The mood of darkness and anxiety continues and truly grounds me as the tracks develop. The National Anthem is probably my favourite track of the album, being so highly elevated by the incredible, jazz-influenced instrumental section that is unlike anything else on the album. How to Disappear Completely slightly disappointed me. It has been commonly crowned as the best song of the album by many critics that I watch and respect, but after truly experiencing it, it was a slight letdown. Again, by no means bad, but my expectations were just too high, which is my own fault, but also the fault of the people who hyped it up. Treefingers brings the second half of the album in a very mellow way that leads into Optimistic. A solid, interesting track, with some cool, for once decipherable lyrics, slightly brought down by the chorus for me. In Limbo is something I should enjoy on paper, as I am a huge fan of classic literature and Dante’s Inferno, but the execution just didn’t really resonate with me. But Idioteque does really hit. The change in pace and percussion, leaning heavily on the elctornic EDM like sound. It just feels like such a nice breath of fresh air and a very much needed break from the gloomy sound, to a more gloomy topic, this one being of a nuclear war and subsequent winter. A topic I am a big fan of, or atleast the concept of. Morning Bell and the closer Motion Picture Soundtrack are both quite interesting and have their own individual personalities, making them worth it in the long run, but as they stand, they just feel like the rest, after being fatigued by the exact same sound for 40 minutes straight.

Overall, the album is definitely good, great even, but from a personal point of view, it falls flat to the high expectations placed upon it by the entire music community. I definitely see the appeal and respect what they have done here, as it is a craft truly worth the masters. But I feel like it thrives from specific environments and total attention, things that I do not have at my disposal at all times, and thus it pales slightly to their other records. There is some solid replay value, and when it hits, it hits hard, but just the fact that I almost feel bored at times when listening to it is something that I did not expect from a Radiohead record. So it’s a really and truly solid album worth the praise, but it just might not be for me.

If OK Computer ran its experiments through band instrumentation, Kid A pulls in genres like electronic, ambient and IDM on top of that, arriving at a sound that is mysterious and experimental at once. It's the kind of sound that could easily turn impenetrable and tedious, but they hold onto a warm, pop sensibility inside it.

The album paints a dreamlike, cosmic space throughout. The synthesizers blanketing that space, the distinctive beat running across the whole record, the bass filling in the balance, and effects you can't quite identify but that are mysterious and beautiful all fill it up. Samples are put to aggressive use and placed with real taste; sounds that could have come off as abrasive are smoothed over well, and the dynamic, explosive passages are handled just as smoothly. Thom Yorke's vocals carry a deeply melancholy feeling, and the parts where they're fragmented and used like an instrument are another point of appeal.

The tracks divide into band-sound songs and electronic ones, and while the album crosses back and forth between the two genres it keeps its unity. Each track's individuality still comes through, and the tone that creates that unity is what I'd call the magic of this album. The electronic tracks layer distinctive beats with mysterious, beautiful samples piled on top, while the band tracks bring in all kinds of effects to express textures unlike a conventional band sound. Personally I'm drawn to the band-sound tracks: the oddly textured repeating bass of "The National Anthem" and the explosive jazz in its back half, or the heavily distorted guitar on "Optimistic," are the examples.

Plenty of albums get so buried in their experiments that they lose the emotional side, but this one manages both at once. I suspect this is the very thing that put Radiohead where they are today. It's an album that can satisfy longtime Radiohead fans and new listeners alike.
