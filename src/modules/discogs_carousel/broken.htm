Anything but punk.
London Calling has been with me my whole life. I can remember hearing the CD playing in the kitchen as a small child; then discovering it amongst my dad’s old record collection in the loft as a teenager (“yes!!”), then learning the art of turning a record over in my bedroom (a double album, so double the practice).

It feels like a best of that spans a band’s whole career, seamlessly delivering you from one era to another - not The Clash’s third LP in three years. It’s crazy to think that so shortly before this album they were legitimately punk. While London Calling explores New Wave, Garage, Ska and Reggae, to my ears there is nothing punk on display here!

While there are well recognised hits throughout this beast (and side B has a particularly high concentration) less celebrated track Hateful has always had a special place in my heart due to its peculiar vocal inflections which show a particularly odd and elaborate side to The Clash. I also love the breezy, super slick choruses of I’m Not Down - another underrated classic!

Way2Tonal Jun 19 2026
Vote downBookmarkVote up
𝕄𝕐 /𝕄𝕌/ 𝕁𝕠𝕦𝕣𝕟𝕖𝕪 ( 278 / 𝟒𝟖𝟎 )
Whenever I discuss my all-time favourite double albums, I am aware that most of them have low points that I tend to overlook because the rest of the record is so fantastic.

Now, this is natural. Making an outstanding piece of work where every track leaves a lasting impression that makes the listener come back frequently is hard enough; stretching that out to an hour plus of content whose quality has to be flawless might be unachievable. That’s why I don’t believe in a “perfect” album. It’s a physical impossibility that goes against what reality is. In my opinion.

And that notion is very much implemented when I’m talking about lengthier releases. There could be many reasons why a double album is not perfect. Sometimes the decent tracks cannot compete with the best tracks on the record or maybe there’s simply a little bit too much filler that I wish wasn’t there. But there’s one oddity in my favourites that doesn’t follow any of the listed reasons above and is the closest an album has come to being a perfect double album: The Clash’s London Calling.

Four Horsemen’s somewhat lazy attempt at a refrain would be the low point that I’d overlook, but due to how impeccably curated every single song is on this album, this track becomes practically invisible. Mick Jones and Joe Strummer feel like such an unstoppable writing force in this crop of songs. They tell these stories of predominantly London youth that praise their alternative stances (Rudie Can’t Fail), but the record is smart enough to address empathy in their misfortune (Lost in the Supermarket) and bold enough to criticize that status quo that wants to use them (Clampdown. They tackle all these aspects of youth alongside excursions that range from the Spanish Civil War on Spanish Bombs to teaching about safe sex on Lover’s Rock that comes off as if the band truly understand the generation they're a part of, which feels so genuine.

But this beautiful consensus on youth is paired with the band’s most impressive genre splicing so far. Gone are the days of their rudimentary punk roots style and in come all these interpretations of ska like on Wrong ‘Em Boyo which sounds great but then aspects of this genre like the horns are spliced alongside other compositions like The Right Profile and The Card Cheat that makes this album feel way bigger than the roots this music originally came from. It’s a completely new evolution in music. An evolution that still pays homage to older acts like the slick Vince Taylor worship on Brand New Cadillac or the multicultural celebration of Danny Ray’s ( Revolution Rock. And obviously I can’t finish this review without mentioning the title-track and Train in Vain. Whilst the former isn’t my favourite song, it is so intrinsic to this album’s track list and popularity that I can’t see this album without it. And the former is a bitter but one last catchy, sing-along hoorah whose harmonica and 5-note guitar hook will be forever stuck in my head.

Punk Rock had already been established years ago through the efforts of rebels like The Damned and Sex Pistols, but the very late 70’s served as the transitional period where this music got more nuanced and atmospheric (i.e. post-punk) and gave birth to so many great bands. However, The Clash decided to take notes from their poppier counterparts in the U.S. and their own local scenes, such as 2-Tone and created a catchy yet sophisticated magnum opus that, pretty much perfectly encapsulates the revolutionary sounds of late 70s U.K.

Also, the moody, nihilistic digression that is Paul Simonon's Guns of Brixton is fantastic also. Quite the standout stylistic change that further proves the talent of the lads.
